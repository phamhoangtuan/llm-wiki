# Spaced Repetition

Spaced repetition is a learning technique that schedules review sessions at increasing intervals — specifically at the point of near-forgetting — to maximize long-term retention. It is the primary counter-strategy to the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve, which shows that memories decay exponentially without reinforcement.

The Forgetting Curve

Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885) demonstrated that memory fades rapidly after initial learning, with the steepest drop occurring within the first 24 hours. Without review, most information is lost within days. Each successful retrieval at the point of near-forgetting _repairs and strengthens_ the memory, flattening the decay curve.

Three Theories of Forgetting

Theory Mechanism Example
Decay Memories fade with time Learn vocabulary → forget after 2 weeks without review
Interference New and old memories compete Proactive: old software shortcuts block new ones. Retroactive: new language syntax overwrites previous language
Forgotten Cues Memory exists but retrieval key is lost "Locked box" — you know you know it, can't access it

Spaced repetition addresses all three: decay by re-encoding before loss, interference by strengthening target memories, and cue loss by varied retrieval contexts.

Optimal Spacing Schedule

Young recommends a practical schedule for self-directed learners:

Interval Review Timing
Initial Day 1 (first learning session)
1st Review Day 2 (24 hours later)
2nd Review Day 7 (1 week later)
3rd Review Day 30 (1 month later)
4th Review Day 90 (3 months later)

The core principle: review at the point of near-forgetting. Too soon, and retrieval is too easy (shallow encoding). Too late, and the memory is already lost (requiring re-learning rather than strengthening).

This aligns with Bjork's concept of desirable difficulty — harder retrieval produces deeper, more durable encoding.

The Scrabble Champion Case

Nigel Richards provides a dramatic demonstration: he memorized the entire French Scrabble dictionary in 9 weeks — without speaking French — and won the French Scrabble World Championship. His method:

  • Active recall during cycling trips (retrieval practice)
  • Rehearsal sessions spread over time (spacing)
  • Allowing memory to weaken before strengthening (near-forgetting)

Tools

Dedicated spaced repetition software automates the scheduling:

  • Anki — Open-source flashcard app using the SM-2 algorithm (derived from SuperMemo)
  • SuperMemo — The original algorithm, developed by Piotr Woźniak, with more sophisticated scheduling
  • Manual tracking — For project-based learning, Young recommends manually scheduling review dates in a calendar

Spacing vs. Cramming

Research consistently shows that spaced practice produces _superior long-term retention_ compared to massed practice (cramming), even when total study time is equal. Cramming may produce short-term performance gains (useful for tomorrow's exam) but results in rapid decay. Spacing trades short-term fluency for durable knowledge.
