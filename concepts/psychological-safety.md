## Summary

**Psychological Safety** is the shared belief that a team member can take interpersonal risks — ask questions, admit mistakes, propose ideas, challenge the status quo — without fear of punishment, embarrassment, or rejection. In Google's **Project Aristotle** study of 180 teams, it ranked as the **#1** dynamic separating high-performing teams from the rest, ahead of dependability, structure, meaning, and impact (source: [[sources/leading-effective-engineering-teams-osmani]]).

## The Project Aristotle Five

Ranked by importance, the five dynamics of high-performing teams:

1. **Psychological Safety** 🛡️ — members dare to take risks and admit errors without fear of punishment (*most important*)
2. **Dependability** — commit to delivering quality work on time
3. **Structure & Clarity** — clear roles, plans, and goals
4. **Meaning** — the work is personally meaningful
5. **Impact** — members believe their work creates change

## Safety Is Not a Soft Skill

- Psychological safety is the **foundation for innovation** — safe teams **admit mistakes early** (before they compound) and **propose risky ideas**
- The "watermelon" failure mode thrives where safety is absent: teams hide the red inside behind green metrics (see [[output-vs-outcome]])
- Google's flat-org experiment **failed** — managers matter, but their role shifted to creating safety and enabling autonomy rather than controlling work

## How Leaders Build It

- **Model vulnerability** — admit your own mistakes first; the manager sets the norm
- **Replace criticism with curiosity** — ask "Why not?" instead of judging (see [[behavioral-habits]])
- **Coach rather than dictate** — question-based feedback signals it is safe to think out loud (see [[coaching]])
- **Standard co-creation** — Osmani's finding: letting the team co-author standards raised effectiveness ~23% (see [[multiplier-leadership]])
- **Protect team values** — never tolerate bullying or toxic behavior (see [[engineering-management]])
