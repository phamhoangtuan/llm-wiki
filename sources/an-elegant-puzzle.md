## Summary

Will Larson presents engineering management as a systems discipline: the manager's primary work is not producing code directly, but designing the conditions in which teams can produce sustainable results. The book follows the transition from senior engineer to manager, then to manager of managers and organizational leader.

## Core Ideas

### Design for Team Stability

Aim for teams large enough to have resilience and small enough to remain coachable. The notes recommend roughly six to eight engineers as a useful operating range and warn that very small teams become fragile during vacations, departures, and changing priorities. Team design is an engineering decision because structure determines communication load, ownership, and continuity.

### Diagnose Before Prescribing

Teams move through recognizable states: falling behind, treading water, repaying debt, and innovating. Each state needs a different intervention. Hiring is appropriate for a capacity problem; reducing work in progress is better for a focus problem; time and slack are necessary when a team is paying down debt or protecting a healthy system.

### Migrations Are the Scalable Form of Debt Reduction

Technical debt is rarely fixed by a one-off cleanup. Larson's migration playbook is **de-risk, enable, finish**: embed the change with a team, build tooling and paths that make adoption easy, then stop the old path from continuing to accumulate users. See [[technical-debt-management]].

### Clarity, Policy, and Culture

A manager's "no" should explain constraints and priorities rather than merely reject a request. Work the policy instead of granting endless exceptions; every exception creates exception debt. Culture is the accumulated record of organizational behavior, so inclusion, peer loyalty, humane interviews, career ladders, and rejecting heroics must be expressed in repeated operating practices.

### Scale the Operating System

As scope grows, managers need feedback redundancy: staff meetings, skip-level one-on-ones, dashboards with directional metrics, and deliberate delegation. The manager is better understood as a gardener who shapes structure, resources, and direction than as a mechanic who directly controls every outcome.
